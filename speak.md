Patrick Winston delivered his famous How to Speak lecture at MIT for over 40 years. The 2018 version, approximately one hour long, focuses on making ideas understandable, persuasive, and memorable.

Source: [MIT OpenCourseWare — Full lecture](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/res-tll-005-how-to-speak-january-iap-2018/pages/how-to-speak/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

## The central message

Winston's central argument is that your ability to communicate ideas is just as important as the quality of the ideas themselves.

You can have a brilliant idea, but if you cannot explain it clearly, people may never recognize its value.

He offers a collection of practical techniques for teaching, presenting research, passing oral examinations, and succeeding in job interviews.

## 1. Start with an empowerment promise

VIDEO: 04:15 — HOW TO START

Start by telling your audience what they will be able to do or understand after listening.

Avoid beginning with an apology, an irrelevant joke, or a long introduction.

For example:

Weak opening

"Today I'm going to talk about artificial intelligence."

Better opening

"By the end of this presentation, you'll understand three ways AI can improve your team's productivity."

The second version gives the audience a reason to pay attention.

## 2. Use four techniques to make ideas memorable

VIDEO: 05:38 — FOUR SAMPLE HEURISTICS

These are among the most useful techniques in the lecture.

| Technique          | What it means                                 | How to apply it                                                       |
| ------------------ | --------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Cycle              | Repeat an important idea in different ways    | Explain it, illustrate it, and revisit it later                       |
| Build a fence      | Explain what an idea is and what it is not    | Contrast correct examples with similar but incorrect ones             |
| Verbal punctuation | Signal transitions clearly                    | Say "First...", "Here's the key point...", or "Now let's consider..." |
| Ask questions      | Re-engage the audience and stimulate thinking | Ask a meaningful question, then allow several seconds for an answer   |

For example, if you're explaining machine learning, don't just define it. Show what machine learning does, contrast it with conventional programming, and revisit that distinction during a practical example.

Winston particularly emphasizes repetition because audiences rarely remember an unfamiliar concept after hearing it only once.

## 3. Control the environment

VIDEO: 10:17 — TIME AND PLACE

Winston recommends choosing conditions that help people remain attentive.

- Schedule presentations when people are alert. He suggests around 11 a.m. as a good time.
- Choose a well-lit room so people remain awake and can see you.
- Visit the room beforehand and test the equipment.
- Speak in a space that feels appropriately populated rather than unnecessarily empty.

He also recommends keeping your hands visible and using natural gestures rather than hiding them in your pockets.

## 4. Use slides, whiteboards, and props intelligently

VIDEO: 13:24 — BOARDS, PROPS, AND SLIDES

One of Winston's strongest points is that PowerPoint is not always the best teaching tool.

He prefers physical demonstrations and boards when they help ideas develop naturally.

PowerPoint slides

Keep them simple, eliminate unnecessary words, use clear graphics, and never merely read the slides aloud.

Winston's guiding principle is that visual aids should help the audience follow your reasoning, not compete with your voice for attention.

## 5. Distinguish between informing and persuading

VIDEO: 36:30–41:30

Winston makes an important distinction between two purposes of public speaking.

When informing an audience, you should provide a clear promise, stimulate curiosity, and teach people a useful way of thinking about the subject.

When persuading an audience, such as in an oral examination, research defense, or job interview, you must also demonstrate why your work or viewpoint deserves attention.

For research and job talks, he emphasizes establishing two things early:

- Vision: What important problem are you trying to solve, and what is distinctive about your approach?
- Accomplishment: What have you actually done to advance that vision?

He recommends establishing both in roughly the first five minutes.

## 6. Make your ideas memorable: the Five S's

In discussing how ideas become widely recognized, Winston introduces five useful elements.

Symbol

A recognizable image or visual identity

Slogan

A short, memorable phrase

Surprise

An unexpected insight

Salient idea

A clear, distinctive central point

Story

Explain how the idea emerged, how it works, and why it matters.

These are especially useful when presenting research, introducing a product, or pitching a proposal.&#x20;

## 7. Finish with your contribution, not just “Thank you”

VIDEO: 53:06 — HOW TO STOP

Winston believes the ending deserves careful preparation.

He discourages ending with a generic “Thank you” slide or allowing the question-and-answer session to be the final impression.

Instead, return to your central contribution and end with a strong, memorable statement.

For example, rather than ending with:

"That's all. Thank you. Any questions?"

You might say:

"We've seen how three small design changes can make this system faster, safer, and easier to maintain. That's the opportunity in front of us."

This reinforces what your audience should remember.

## The 5 most useful lessons to remember

If you remember only five things from this lecture, I would prioritize these:

1. Start with a promise. Tell your audience what they will gain.
2. Repeat important ideas. Explain them through different examples and perspectives.
3. Make complexity understandable. Use contrasts, simple visuals, and demonstrations.
4. Show why your ideas matter. Especially during job interviews or technical presentations.
5. End with a memorable contribution. Make your final message something worth remembering.

## How to apply this in a workplace presentation

Suppose you're presenting a new software solution to your colleagues. Here's how you could apply Winston's approach.

| Stage                 | Example                                                                                                |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Opening promise       | "In the next ten minutes, I'll show you how we can reduce deployment failures."                        |
| Establish the problem | "Currently, our deployment process requires several manual steps."                                     |
| Explain your solution | Show a simple before-and-after workflow                                                                |
| Reinforce the idea    | Demonstrate how automation reduces errors and saves time                                               |
| Final contribution    | "The main benefit isn't just faster deployment. It's a more reliable and predictable release process." |

This approach works particularly well for technical presentations because it helps non-specialists understand the value of your work.

My biggest takeaway from Winston's lecture: Good public speaking isn't primarily about sounding impressive. It's about helping the audience understand, remember, and appreciate the importance of your ideas.
